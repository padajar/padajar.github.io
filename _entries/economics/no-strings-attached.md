  - label: "abstract"
    body: |
      Should universities admit students without considering performance on their final exams? When universities do so, students who accept the offers may underperform for two reasons: students may reduce their academic effort toward the test (moral hazard), or students with low private signals may self-select into accepting offers (private information). We disentangle these forces in the context of the UK's university application system using the staggered rollout of unconditional offers, which allowed some students to matriculate at university without any requirements on their age-18 final exams (A-levels). Students who accept an unconditional offer underperform those who decline one by 0.14 SD on age-18 exams, conditional on observables. Using a two-way fixed effects specification, we find that exposure to unconditional offers has precisely zero impact on students' age-18 exam performance, indicating that the observed gap is driven by private information, not moral hazard. Exposure to unconditional offers increases university matriculation by 1.1 percentage points.

      To determine the overall policy implications, we develop and estimate a structural model to evaluate the spillover effects onto non-recipients, the value students place on the certainty of admission, and the extent of misallocation. We estimate that students know two-thirds of the variance in their realized test scores before taking the test. The median student does not value the certainty of admission. Unconditional offers decrease total student welfare by 0.7% relative to a ban and redistribute welfare from the three highest-achieving quartiles to the lowest-achieving quartile. A shift to post-qualification admissions, under which students apply after receiving their age-18 exam scores, raises student welfare by 12.5% in our simulations. All students except the lowest-achieving quartile benefit, as do all programs except the most-selective quartile. Our results highlight how the distributional effects of admissions policies depend on student information, informing current policy debates in the UK.
