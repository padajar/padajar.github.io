panels:
  - label: "abstract"
    body: |
      High-stakes final exams shape students' educational paths in two ways: universities use different sets of information to screen applicants, shaping the equilibrium *match*, and they may create *incentives* for students to exert effort. We study these forces using the staggered introduction of unconditional offers in the UK, which allowed students to matriculate regardless of results from their age-18 exams (A-levels). We first show there are no incentive effects: while students who accept an unconditional offer score 0.14 SD below those who decline one, conditional on observables, using a two-way fixed effects specification, we find that exposure to unconditional offers has a precisely estimated null effect on students' age-18 exam performance. Unconditional offers have positive match effects for recipients: exposure increases matriculation by 1.1pp, largely at middle-tercile universities. Using a structural model, we find that unconditional offers are anti-meritocratic: they benefit the lowest-achieving quartile of students at the expense of higher-achieving students and lower overall welfare by 0.5%. Post-qualification admissions, under which students apply after receiving their scores, raise welfare by 14.2% through better sorting. This gain is mostly attributable to increased information for both sides of the market. Our results inform current policy debates in the UK, highlighting how the distributional effects of admissions policies depend on student information.
